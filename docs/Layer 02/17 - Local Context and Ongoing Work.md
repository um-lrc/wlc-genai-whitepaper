# Local Context and Ongoing Work

## Situating this work within our program and institution

This document is part of a broader, ongoing effort to support thoughtful teaching and learning in World Languages & Cultures in the context of generative AI. It does not stand alone, and it is not the final word. Rather, it reflects work already underway and invites continued conversation and experimentation.

The sections below outline current and planned activities, resources, and opportunities for engagement. They are descriptive rather than prescriptive, and participation in any of these efforts is voluntary.

---

## A. Workshops and Professional Development

The LRC's engagement with AI and educational technology in language learning stretches back well before generative AI became a widespread institutional concern. What follows is a roughly chronological account of where that work has taken place and what it has looked like.

**Early exploration (2018–2022).** LRC staff were attending and presenting at IALLT (International Association for Language Learning Technology) and its regional affiliates by at least 2018, when the LRC was preparing proposals for IALLT 2019 focused on AI-assisted language learning — including early experiments with voice recognition hardware (Google AIY Voice and Vision kits) — and attended that conference in Oregon. The LRC has maintained connections with the Michigan-area language technology community (MiALLT/MWALLT) continuously since that period.

**Enriching Scholarship (2023–present).** LRC staff co-organized and moderated an Enriching Scholarship 2023 panel titled *Busting a Move: Empowering Instructors to Re-Imagine Teaching and Learning*, which brought together faculty and administrators from across all three U-M campuses to discuss what was then the just-emerging challenge of generative AI in the classroom. That panel followed a keynote by Cynthia Alby specifically on AI and educational disruption. At Enriching Scholarship 2025, a session on "digital brain" tools and personal knowledge management was warmly received by attendees. At Enriching Scholarship 2026, LRC staff will present on this guide itself and on Extempore as an oral assessment tool for language courses.

**IALLT and external conferences (2023–2025).** LRC staff participated in an IALLT webinar in December 2023 on generative AI in language learning, one of the first structured conversations in the language technology community specifically focused on that topic. At the 2025 IALLT/FLEAT 8 conference in Minnesota (June 2025), LRC staff attended and presented. LRC staff also attended the AI in the Humanities conference in Knoxville (April 2025), the Digital Pedagogy Institute (August 2024), and the MAALLT Tech Slam (August 2024). LRC staff have served as mentors through IALLT for newer professionals in the field.

**Campus workshops and departmental sessions (2023–present).** The LRC has offered workshops and drop-in sessions for instructors and GSIs across many language programs since 2023, increasingly with AI-adjacent topics woven in. Topics have included oral assessment with **Extempore**, AI-assisted exercise creation with **H5P and Smart Import**, **NotebookLM** for document synthesis and podcast generation, **vocabulary with songs and images**, **podcasting and audio essays**, and a session called **Reimagining Journalling** exploring how reflective writing assignments can be redesigned in an AI-present classroom. Departmental and program-level sessions have been offered to instructors in Chinese, Japanese, Korean (SALP), Southeast Asian, South Asian, Germanic, Classical Studies, RLL (French, Italian, Spanish), Comp Lit, ELI, and Slavic, typically at the request of program coordinators and tailored to the program's specific questions and materials. The Teaching Techniques and Strategies Workshop (German Department, May 2026) will include LRC presentations on Reimagining Journalling and Extempore.

**Translate-a-thon.** The LRC's annual Translate-a-thon — a multi-day community translation marathon held each October in North Quad — has explicitly engaged with AI in translation as a theme. The 2024 event (October 18–19, with 119 participants and 28 languages represented) was organized around the theme "AI and Translation" and opened with a keynote address titled *AI in Translation: For Better or Worse?* delivered by Bridget Hylak, a language industry veteran, AI ethics consultant, and contributor to federal guidance on machine interpreting. Hylak's talk examined both the productivity gains AI translation tools offer and the risks — biased outputs, hallucinations, cultural insensitivity, and data security — and argued for an expert-in-the-loop model in which human linguists and AI tools work in collaboration rather than substitution. The event also included an "En Nuestra Lengua" session for heritage speakers and a collaboration with students from partner institution CUCEA (Mexico). The 2025 Translate-a-thon (November 7–8) continued this programming. The 2026 event is planned for October 30–31. The Translate-a-thon is an accessible entry point for language instructors and students curious about AI and translation, and recordings of keynote addresses have been made available to participants after each event.

**Campus-wide engagements.** LRC staff also participated in the Provost's Seminar on Teaching with Generative AI (November 2025), staffing a resource table during the concurrent session block; the LSA Humanities Faculty Conversations on GenAI (January 2026); and the Library's panel on AI in Language Instruction (April 2026). The LRC organized and convened the Big Ten Language Summit (fall 2024), which included working groups on topics relevant to AI's intersection with language program advocacy, proficiency standards, and K–12 connections.

> These workshops are designed to support exploration, shared problem-solving, and pedagogical reflection, not to mandate specific practices or tools.

## B. Tools and Technologies Under Exploration

The following represents a sample of what LRC staff and interested instructors have been actively experimenting with. Inclusion here does not imply endorsement, institutional requirement, or recommendation for all contexts.

**Oral practice and assessment.** [Extempore](https://extemporeapp.com/) has been the most actively explored tool for supporting and assessing spoken language production. It allows instructors to create scaffolded, multi-prompt oral assignments that students complete asynchronously by audio recording. The LRC is using it as a replacement for DiLL-based oral assessments and supporting instructors interested in shifting away from written proxies for speaking skill. Canvas integration is pending ITS review.

**Conversational AI for practice.** LRC staff have been building custom conversational voice applications using [Google AI Studio](https://aistudio.google.com/) and the Gemini API, including a prototype for ELI academic conversation practice and a collaboration with Arabic language instructors on a custom chatbot grounded in course vocabulary and grammar. These projects raise practical questions about API costs, FERPA, and deployment that the team is actively working through with U-M IT contacts. [Mizou](https://mizou.com/) is a simpler instructor-facing tool that does not require students to create accounts and is available at a free tier; it is currently recommended when instructors want to experiment with conversation bots without the complexity of custom development.

**Interactive exercises and vocabulary tools.** [H5P](https://h5p.org/), available through LSA's institutional license at [um-lsa.h5p.com](https://um-lsa.h5p.com), is the primary tool being developed as a replacement for Quizlet, which is no longer supported due to accessibility and privacy concerns. H5P's Smart Import feature uses AI to generate multiple activity types (dialog cards, crosswords, question sets, drag-the-words exercises) from a vocabulary list in a single operation. LRC staff have developed workflows and an internal batch-generation tool to assist with the conversion process.

**GenAI tools available campus-wide.** [UM-GPT](https://umgpt.umich.edu/) and [Maizey](https://umgpt.umich.edu/maizey/) are U-M's institutionally supported AI platforms. Maizey allows instructors to build RAG-based (Retrieval Augmented Generation) course assistants grounded in their own course materials. [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com/) is now available through U-M Google accounts and has been explored for generating study guides, podcast-style audio overviews, and connections across document sets. These tools are most useful for single-turn queries rather than sustained conversation and work best when instructors give students explicit guidance on their appropriate use and limitations.

**Music and creative generation.** Suno (AI music generation) has been used by LRC staff and some instructors to create songs that reinforce vocabulary or grammar patterns, particularly for beginning-level courses. The approach is more useful as a demonstration of AI's creative capacities and limitations than as a reliable instructional tool.

**Accessibility tools (connected to AI).** The LSA-configured [Alt Text Generator](https://gemini.google.com/gem/16l1p4Z1EuHU6_KNf5COfGATtsO4VbcBi) (a Gemini-based tool) generates image alt text following LSA's guidelines for web and document accessibility. [Panorama](https://panorama.yuja.com/) (YuJa's accessibility platform, available through Canvas) and [Grackle](https://www.grackledocs.com/) (for Google Docs and Slides) are used by LRC staff in supporting language instructors through the digital accessibility work required by April 2026 Title II compliance deadlines.

The purpose of this exploration is to better understand how different tools intersect with learning goals in language education, not to promote universal adoption.

## C. Opportunities for Conversation and Experimentation

**Support and Share Space.** LRC holds weekly drop-in hours at multiple campus locations (Modern Languages Building, South Thayer Building, East Quad, and online via Zoom), Monday through Friday throughout the semester. These are open to any language instructor who wants to talk through a course design question, get hands-on help with a tool, or simply think out loud about something they are encountering in their teaching. No appointment is needed and no specific agenda is required. Current hours and locations are posted on the [LRC website](https://lsa.umich.edu/lrc/language-teaching.html).

**LSA Faculty Conversations on Generative AI.** LSA has hosted a series of faculty conversations organized by division, including a Humanities session in January 2026 at which LRC staff served as table scribes. These sessions are intended for instructors to share what they are experiencing and hearing from students, and have surfaced themes that directly inform the work described in this guide. If you are interested in participating in future sessions, watch for announcements through your department or the TTC.

**Departmental share fairs and program-level sessions.** Several departments — including RLL, Germanic Languages, Classical Studies, SALP, and ELI — have organized internal share fairs or discussion sessions where instructors present approaches they are testing in their courses, with LRC staff participating as facilitators and discussants. If your program would like to host a conversation of this kind, the LRC can help organize and facilitate it.

**Language Summit.** The International Institute's annual Language Summit (December 2025) brought together faculty, staff, and students from across U-M's language programs to discuss the state of language education at the university, with AI as a significant theme. LRC staff participated as panelists. The summit is a regular venue for cross-program conversation that the LRC supports.

**Enriching Scholarship.** The annual Enriching Scholarship conference (May, at the LRC) is an opportunity for instructors to share pedagogical experiments and hear from colleagues across programs. LRC staff have presented at recent sessions on topics including oral assessment, AI in language instruction, and this guide itself.

**Pilot projects and low-stakes experimentation.** Several instructors have worked with LRC staff on small pilot projects: testing a conversational AI tool with a group of students, redesigning a single assignment to build in more oral production, or converting a Quizlet deck to H5P. These do not require formal proposals or institutional approval, and the LRC can provide practical support, help think through the questions worth asking, and assist with documenting what was learned.

Participation in these activities is optional and intended to support collegial exchange rather than evaluation.

## D. Support, Consultation, and Collaboration

**LRC direct support.** The LRC's instructional staff are available for individual consultations on any of the topics in this guide: assignment redesign, oral assessment options, AI policy language, tool selection, digital accessibility, or simply talking through a situation you are uncertain about. The best starting point is [lrc.contact@umich.edu](mailto:lrc.contact@umich.edu), or stopping by Support and Share Space hours without an appointment.

**Instructional grants.** LSA Technology Services administers [NINI grants](https://lsa.umich.edu/technology-services/services/funding-grants/nini-grants.html) and [Level I grants](https://lsa.umich.edu/technology-services/services/funding-grants/level-I.html) that support language instructors in developing new course materials, building interactive exercises, or piloting technology-enhanced approaches. Several ongoing projects in Spanish, Chinese, Ojibwe, African languages, and SALP have been funded through these mechanisms, with LRC providing implementation support. If you have a project in mind, the LRC can help you scope it and identify the right funding channel.

**LSA Tech Services.** LSA Technology Services Teaching and Learning colleagues collaborate closely with LRC on software licensing, accessibility reviews, Canvas support, and instructional design. If you have questions about using a particular tool, requesting an accessibility review, or navigating the Canvas integration process for third-party apps, their team can be reached via a [Course Pedagogical Consultation ticket](https://teamdynamix.umich.edu/TDClient/47/LSAPortal/Requests/TicketRequests/NewForm?ID=4SrtLtea83g_&RequestorType=ServiceOffering).

**LSA GenAI Center of Excellence.** LSA's Generative AI Center of Excellence offers in-person workshops for LSA staff on topics including getting started with UM-GPT and Gemini, prompt writing, and tools such as NotebookLM and Gems. These sessions are available to instructional staff and are listed in the student life scheduling system. If your unit would like a tailored presentation, the team accepts requests through LSA's TDX portal.

**ITS Teaching and Learning.** ITS offers a recurring [weekly GenAI workshop series](https://its.umich.edu/teaching-learning/workshops/training) on Thursdays (12:00–1:00 PM, Zoom), covering introductions to UM-GPT, best practices, and emerging use cases. Sessions are recorded and available on demand.

Support is available to help instructors think through choices in ways that align with their courses, values, and constraints.

## E. A Note on Autonomy and Ongoing Revision

It is important to emphasize that engagement with generative AI — and with the activities described above — is **not required**. Faculty retain full autonomy over their teaching practices and course policies.

This document, and the work it describes, should be understood as evolving. As tools, institutional contexts, and pedagogical conversations continue to change, so too will our approaches. Feedback, questions, and alternative perspectives are welcome and expected.

*This section is intended to make local work visible, not to define a single path forward.*
