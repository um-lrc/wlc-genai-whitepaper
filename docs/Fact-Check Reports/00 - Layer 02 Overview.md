## Strongest Empirical Anchors Across Layer 2

The following research traditions provide especially strong support for Layer 2's arguments. These are the most useful anchor literatures.

**Working students, financial precarity, and academic outcomes.** Andrews & Lucas (2022) on financial stress and community college outcomes; Ecton et al. (2023) on 17 years of Tennessee statewide records; Hart (2017) for the "precarity" theoretical framework; Joo et al. (2008) and House et al. (2020) for the foundational link to academic performance.

**Cognitive offloading and AI dependency.** Gerlich (2025) on AI tool use and critical thinking (666 participants); Skulmowski (2023) on the "illusion of having memorized"; Matueny et al. (2025) on the "illusion of competence" framing; Elzerman (2025) for direct application to language learning.

**Retrieval practice and desirable difficulty.** Roediger & Butler (2011), Karpicke & Roediger (2007), Bjork & Bjork (2011), and Bjork & Kroll (2015) for vocabulary-specific extension. Aljabri (2024) extends the retrieval-practice finding directly to EFL contexts.

**Distributed cognition.** Hutchins (1995, *Cognition in the Wild*) and Zhang & Norman (1994) are the canonical theoretical anchors. Deitrick & Reeves (2015) provides direct classroom-application evidence.

**Image generation bias.** Bianchi et al. (2022, 397 citations) is the most-cited study on text-to-image stereotyping; Cheong et al. (2024), Sun et al. (2023), Górska & Jemielniak (2023), Aldahoul et al. (2024), Ali et al. (2023), and Naik & Nushi (2023) all corroborate with specific numerical findings.

**Common Crawl composition and LLM English-centrism.** Mehmood et al. (2017) on the 60.6% English figure; Dodge et al. (2021) on C4 documentation; Caswell et al. (2021) on multilingual corpus quality audits; Ranaldi et al. (2023) and Zhou et al. (2024) on English-centric LLM behavior.

**LLM training and inference energy/carbon.** Strubell et al. (2019) for foundational training estimates; Luccioni et al. (2022) for life-cycle BLOOM analysis; Chien et al. (2023, 2024) for the inference-dominance finding; Kaur et al. (2026) for per-query domain-level breakdowns; Stojkovic et al. (2024) on inference cluster optimization.

**Data center water consumption.** Mytton (2021) is the foundational peer-reviewed study (data centers use 0.14% of total US water consumption; less than 1/3 of operators measure water use); Karimi et al. (2022) for Phoenix-area trade-off study; Li et al. (2025) for AI-specific water analysis.

**Data center environmental justice.** Cartwright (2026) directly on environmental justice and community impacts; Ngata & Sridharan (2025) on Northern Virginia "Data Center Alley"; Sovacool et al. (2022) on Nordic community impacts; Brodie (2023) for theoretical framing.

**AI labor practices.** Muldoon et al. (2023) directly on Sama operations in Kenya and Uganda (the same company that did content moderation for OpenAI); Tubaro et al. (2019) on micro-work and AI; Miceli & Posada (2021) on power-aware critique of ML data labor.

## Recommended Wording Adjustments

A small number of claims could be tightened to track the empirical evidence more precisely. None are accuracy errors; all are calibration adjustments.

**Chapter 13, Claim 4 — fewer assignments produce stronger evidence.** Braga et al. (2024) found that *more distributed* assessments (frequent, smaller) were perceived as more accurate evaluations of learning. Suggested clarification: "Courses that emphasize fewer compliance-oriented assignments and more sustained engagement..." This makes the assessment-redesign argument without an overclaim on volume.

**Chapter 14, Claim 7 — foundational vs advanced vulnerability hierarchy.** Theoretically grounded in DeKeyser (2017) and consistent with cognitive offloading research, but not directly tested in language learning. Suggested addition: "as the cognitive offloading literature suggests, though direct comparative evidence is still emerging."

**Chapter 15, Claim 2 — "60% or more of web traffic."** Approximately correct but technically imprecise. Suggested replacement: "Estimates suggest that the majority of web text used to train major LLMs is in English (over 60% of documents in the widely-used Common Crawl corpus)." This is precise about what the number measures.

**Chapter 15, Claim 4 — heritage speakers in detection bias.** As noted in earlier reports, the detection-bias literature has tested non-native speakers but has not separately tested heritage speakers. Suggested adjustment: "multilingual writers and others whose L2 prose differs from corpus-typical native English."

**Chapter 16, Claim 2 — training is not a one-time event.** Correct, but reads as 2022-era framing. Recent research (Chien et al. 2023, Kaur et al. 2026) shows inference dominates training in cumulative emissions by ~25x for ChatGPT-like services. Suggested addition: "Recent research suggests that for the most widely used services, the cumulative inference cost has come to exceed training costs by an order of magnitude or more." This *strengthens* the chapter's "ongoing burden" argument.

**Chapter 16, Claim 4 — "few web searches" comparison.** Specific industry estimates put a Google search at ~0.3 Wh vs. ChatGPT queries at 2-3 Wh (a 6-10x ratio rather than "a few"). The chapter's hedging covers this but a knowledgeable reviewer may want it tightened.

## Recurring Patterns and Structural Observations

Three patterns appear across multiple Layer 2 chapters and warrant attention.

**Bibliography gaps.** Files 13 and 15 have no bibliography despite making many specific empirical claims. File 14 has the strongest bibliography in Layer 2 (with a typo: "Bibliograpahy"). File 16 has a strong bibliography that could be expanded with Mytton (2021) and Chien et al. (2023). Across the four chapters, **chapter 15 is the highest-priority candidate for bibliography addition** because it is the most empirically claim-dense.

**Document-specific terminology.** Several Layer 2 framings are the document's own coinages, well-formed but not yet established in the literature. "Academic rigor / logistical rigor" (chapter 13) parallels Kusurkar et al. (2023) on autonomous vs controlled motivation; "purposeful non-use" (chapter 14) is consistent with the cognitive offloading literature but is its own term. The chapters handle these carefully, introducing them as framings rather than established constructs.

**The chapters that handle politically charged topics best are the ones with the most explicit hedging.** Chapter 16 (Climate) is the most carefully hedged chapter in either layer, and it is also the one the user has flagged as held back for expert review. The hedging is empirically defensible; the gap is current literature engagement, not epistemic stance.

## Recurring Research Gaps

Four research gaps recur across Layer 2 chapters and may warrant noting in the document's research-implications discussions.

**Direct comparison of AI's effects on foundational vs advanced language-learning activities.** The vulnerability hierarchy in chapter 14 (and implicit in chapter 13) is theoretically grounded but not yet empirically tested at this level of specificity in language learning.

**Comparative-effectiveness studies of detection-led vs trust-based assessment regimes.** The argument in chapter 13 (and earlier in Layer 1) that trust-based approaches outperform detection-based ones is supported by the surrounding literature but not yet by direct comparison.

**Whole-lifecycle environmental assessments of major commercial AI providers.** Chapter 16's claims rest largely on academic studies of open-source models (BLOOM, OPT, LLaMA) because commercial providers do not consistently disclose comparable data. This limits the ability to make confident commercial-AI-specific claims.

**Language-learning-specific environmental research.** No peer-reviewed study has, to our knowledge, directly examined the environmental costs of AI use in language teaching as opposed to general education or commercial use. This is a tractable research opportunity for the discipline.

## Special Note on Chapter 16 (Climate)

The user has flagged chapter 16 as held back pending expert review, given the politically charged nature of the AI/climate discourse. The accuracy review confirms:

1. **The chapter's claims are empirically defensible.** None of the ten specific claims examined was contradicted by the peer-reviewed literature.
2. **The chapter's epistemic humility is well-calibrated.** Phrases like "the data simply does not support confident claims in either direction" accurately characterize the state of the literature.
3. **Two specific 2023-2026 findings warrant the chapter's engagement before publication.** Inference now dominates training in cumulative emissions (Chien et al. 2023, Kaur et al. 2026, Moore et al. 2025); per-output LLM-vs-human-labor comparisons may favor LLMs (Ren et al. 2024 in *Scientific Reports*). Both findings *strengthen* the chapter's argument and humility rather than weakening them.
4. **The Mytton (2021) citation should be added** as the foundational peer-reviewed water study. It anchors several of the chapter's water claims directly.

A knowledgeable expert reviewer will likely flag the inference-dominance finding first; engaging it explicitly is the highest-leverage pre-publication revision.
