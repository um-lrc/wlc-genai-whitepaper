# Accuracy Review: 14 - On Cognitive Offloading, Deskilling, and Language Learning

**Reviewed:** 2026-05-05
**Claims examined:** 8
**Overall verdict:** Well-supported

---

## Summary

This is the most empirically dense Layer 2 chapter, and it holds up well. Each of its central claims rests on a substantial research tradition: cognitive offloading (Risko, Gilbert, Skulmowski, Armitage), desirable difficulty (Bjork), disuse (Bjork's New Theory of Disuse), retrieval practice (Roediger and Karpicke, Karpicke), distributed cognition (Hutchins, Zhang), and the SLA literature on internalization and procedural knowledge already documented in earlier reports. The chapter is appropriately humble about what the evidence currently establishes — explicitly acknowledging that "research in this area is still emerging" and that "we do not yet have long-term evidence about how extensive AI-supported learning affects language development over time." Its design heuristics are practical extensions of the underlying research rather than overclaims. The bibliography is the strongest in Layer 2, although it could be expanded to anchor a few more specific claims.

## Claim-by-Claim Review

### Claim 1: Cognitive offloading is the use of external tools to reduce mental effort; offloading is not new
> "Cognitive offloading: the use of external tools to reduce the mental effort required to complete a task. Offloading itself is not new. Writing, calculators, dictionaries, and search engines have long functioned as cognitive supports."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Armitage et al. (2024) describe cognitive offloading as humans routinely using "external thinking tools, like pencil and paper, maps, and calculators, to solve cognitive problems that would have once been solved internally." [The nature and development of cognitive offloading in children](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8aa4f7ed3feb5d34a80da27464f5d57d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Skulmowski (2023) defines technology-based cognitive offloading as "using the environment to outsource mental computation" and traces its long history. [The Cognitive Architecture of Digital Externalization](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c866680bf075553c83e92a5185f97d81/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Bulley et al. (2020) extend the framework to children, demonstrating that even 4-year-olds engage in cognitive offloading and that the strategy follows similar principles to adult use. [Children Devise and Selectively Use Tools to Offload Cognition](https://consensus.app/papers/details/05ff1697cb4d5dfdb1a8f8c96bff02a0/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Gilbert et al. (2022) review "intention offloading" specifically, with strong evidence that this strategy is well-established and metacognitively guided. [Outsourcing Memory to External Tools: A Review of 'Intention Offloading'](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d165bf2412345797b275c97a8588dffd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Solid. The historical framing (calculators, dictionaries, writing as cognitive offloading) is consistent with the cognitive psychology literature.

### Claim 2: What is new about generative AI is the scope, fluency, and apparent completeness with which it can perform complex tasks end-to-end
> "What is new is the **scope, fluency, and apparent completeness** of generative AI, which can now perform complex linguistic and analytical tasks end-to-end."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Skulmowski (2023) explicitly distinguishes recent AI from earlier offloading technologies: digital storage and now generative AI create "the illusion of having memorized that information" while underlying memory remains weak. The framing of "scope" and "completeness" aligns with this analysis. [The Cognitive Architecture of Digital Externalization](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c866680bf075553c83e92a5185f97d81/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Matueny et al. (2025) explicitly identifies "the illusion of competence, a misleading perception of mastery created by AI-generated outputs that mask underlying cognitive deficits" — supporting the chapter's "apparent completeness" framing. [Illusion of Competence and Skill Degradation in Artificial Intelligence Dependency among Users](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a98917a1497b56a0829d051456557623/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Rao (2025) summarizes the recent literature: "AI assistance frequently improves immediate task performance while simultaneously undermining durable skill acquisition," with the magnitude of effect distinguishing AI from earlier tools. [The Impact of Artificial Intelligence Tools on Human Cognitive Abilities](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f340fa988958564f82396583e51eb82d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Well-grounded. The chapter's distinction between AI and earlier offloading technologies is consistent with the recent cognitive offloading literature.

### Claim 3: Concerns about deskilling and cognitive atrophy are real but the research is still emerging
> "This shift has prompted concerns about **deskilling** or **cognitive atrophy**, the possibility that frequent reliance on AI may weaken foundational skills when tools replace, rather than support, formative practice. Research in this area is still emerging, and conclusions remain provisional."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:** This claim is well-supported and the chapter's epistemic caution is appropriate.
- Gerlich (2025) provides the clearest direct evidence: "significant negative correlation between frequent AI tool usage and critical thinking abilities, mediated by increased cognitive offloading" in 666 participants. [AI Tools in Society: Impacts on Cognitive Offloading and the Future of Critical Thinking](https://consensus.app/papers/details/886650bd2bc550a49677a8ce40a93d10/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Li et al. (2025) found that GenAI "demonstrates strong potential to enhance learning outcomes and self-efficacy but negatively affects long-term knowledge transfer" with "excessive reliance on GenAI and cognitive outsourcing" impeding effective knowledge acquisition. [Generative artificial intelligence-supported programming education](https://consensus.app/papers/details/58de0096d2da58cfab4a6611f8734050/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Rohilla (2025) found in mixed-methods research with 300+ undergraduates that "high AI dependency" correlated with 17.3 percentage points lower critical thinking scores and 22% fewer concepts retained. [Impact of Excessive AI Tool Usage on the Cognitive Abilities of Undergraduate Students](https://consensus.app/papers/details/bd1dd62b78aa5f4ba6ce38245db33366/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Matueny et al. (2025) develop the "skill degradation" argument from cognitive load theory and technological dependency theory. [Illusion of Competence and Skill Degradation](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a98917a1497b56a0829d051456557623/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** The chapter's "research is still emerging, and conclusions remain provisional" hedge is well-calibrated. Direct evidence is accumulating but most studies are correlational, cross-sectional, or short-term, justifying the chapter's epistemic caution.

### Claim 4: Learning outcomes depend less on the presence of tools than on where difficulty is located and what kinds of effort learners are asked to sustain
> "What does appear consistently, however, is that learning outcomes depend less on the presence of tools than on **where difficulty is located** and **what kinds of effort learners are asked to sustain**."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Bjork and Bjork (2011) is the foundational citation already in the chapter's bibliography: "Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way" articulates the framework that difficulty placement, not tool presence, drives learning. [Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e7fef89288255cb090540e0a025dd48a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Pyke et al. (2024) explicitly compare desirable difficulties framework, retrieval effort hypothesis, and cognitive load theory, concluding that "task difficulty should be adjusted based on the material's complexity and the learner's expertise." This supports the chapter's "where difficulty is located" framing as a generalizable principle. [Does difficulty moderate learning?](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2f022421c11f58ffa44eb20dcf1814ee/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Rao (2025) systematic review concluded that "AI tools do not inherently impair or enhance cognition; rather, their impact depends critically on implementation design, user agency, and interaction patterns" — direct support for the chapter's claim. [The Impact of Artificial Intelligence Tools on Human Cognitive Abilities](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f340fa988958564f82396583e51eb82d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** This is a well-grounded synthesis claim that reflects current expert consensus across multiple research traditions.

### Claim 5: Some forms of difficulty (retrieval, effortful recall, generating language under constraint) are conditions for learning
> "In learning research, this distinction is often discussed through the lens of *desirable difficulties* and *disuse*. Some forms of difficulty, such as retrieval, effortful recall, and the need to generate language under constraint, are not obstacles to learning but conditions for it."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:** The retrieval-practice literature is among the most empirically robust in cognitive psychology.
- Roediger and Butler (2011) is canonical: "retrieval practice is actually a powerful mnemonic enhancer, often producing large gains in long-term retention relative to repeated studying." [The critical role of retrieval practice in long-term retention](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0207e35356b255738a4e6ae7e73e544d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Karpicke and Roediger (2007) demonstrated that "repeated retrieval of information is the key to long-term retention," with retention enhancements over 100% in some conditions. [Repeated retrieval during learning is the key to long-term retention](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0c62e21a43905e24b89c173a61f719ac/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Jonsson et al. (2020) showed the testing effect is independent of cognitive ability — making retrieval-based difficulty broadly applicable. [A learning method for all: The testing effect is independent of cognitive ability](https://consensus.app/papers/details/81fddb4ddf245defb7b9f1873c7a6a40/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Aljabri (2024) extended retrieval practice findings to EFL students: testing produced significantly better long-term retention than repeated study. [Investigating the Effect of Retrieval Practice on EFL Students' Retention of Prose Passages](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7c1073bd94ff532d8d7b3ce54f756398/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Bjork and Kroll (2015), already cited in earlier reports, extends desirable difficulty specifically to vocabulary and language learning. [Desirable Difficulties in Vocabulary Learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d446782fcb4c5711885ec991a874de37/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** This is the chapter's most empirically robust claim — the retrieval-practice and desirable-difficulty literature is unusually deep and well-replicated. The Aljabri (2024) study is particularly relevant because it demonstrates the effect specifically in EFL contexts.

### Claim 6: Tools can extend learning productively when learners remain responsible for interpretation and judgment; problems arise when tools absorb the cognitive work learners are meant to practice (distributed cognition framework)
> "This distinction aligns with broader work on distributed cognition. Tools can extend learning productively when learners remain responsible for interpretation and judgment. Problems arise when tools absorb the very cognitive work learners are meant to practice."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Zhang and Norman (1994) is the canonical empirical statement of distributed cognition theory: "the representational system of a distributed cognitive task is a set of internal and external representations, which together represent the abstract structure of the task." [Representations in Distributed Cognitive Tasks](https://consensus.app/papers/details/5da5bb5e71fe5ef591f4ce7a09998eef/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Zhang and Patel (2006) develop the distributed cognition framework explicitly and demonstrate its applicability to learning. [Distributed cognition, representation, and affordance](https://consensus.app/papers/details/bca4a0a496ec53079d457b2a181c4b18/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Pakdaman-Savoji et al. (2019) systematic review traces "the theory of cognitive tools vis-à-vis learning and development" from Vygotsky through to current educational technology, supporting the chapter's framing. [The conceptualisation of cognitive tools in learning and technology: A review](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e5318709adc356fab1910025a1861f9b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Deitrick and Reeves (2015) apply Hutchins' distributed cognition theory directly to classroom learning, showing that tools can either organize work productively or absorb the cognitive work students should be practicing — a direct empirical analog to the chapter's framing. [Using Distributed Cognition Theory to Analyze Collaborative Computer Science Learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3cf82b267f6c51599e9c32217d23bc15/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Well-grounded. Hutchins' distributed cognition framework is the canonical theoretical anchor here. Adding a Hutchins or Zhang citation to the chapter's bibliography would strengthen this claim directly.

### Claim 7: Foundational language activities (early writing, spontaneous speech, basic translation, retrieval-based practice) are particularly vulnerable to premature offloading; advanced activities focused on analysis or interpretation may be more resilient
> "Foundational activities, including early writing, spontaneous speech, basic translation, and retrieval-based practice, are particularly vulnerable to premature offloading. Later-stage or advanced activities, where the learning goal shifts toward analysis, interpretation, or meta-awareness, may be more resilient and may even benefit from carefully constrained AI use."

**Support level:** Partially supported

**Evidence:** The vulnerability hierarchy is consistent with the literature but has not been directly tested.
- DeKeyser (2017), cited in earlier reports, supports the idea that early-stage L2 learning is "both declarative and explicit" while later-stage learning involves automatization through practice. The chapter's claim that foundational activities are most vulnerable to offloading is consistent with this developmental sequence. [Knowledge and Skill in ISLA](https://consensus.app/papers/details/45d236c2e33b552ebef9af8bd728bd8f/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Hong et al. (2025), cited in earlier reports, demonstrated that advanced (critical thinking) tasks can benefit from AI use when paired with reflection — supporting the second half of the claim that advanced activities may be "more resilient." [Cognitive Offload Instruction with Generative AI](https://consensus.app/papers/details/78794fc5cb445c3ca4672712d936d817/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Sakaguchi et al. (2025), cited in earlier reports, demonstrated that AI handles descriptive themes well but struggles with culturally embedded interpretation — partially supporting the claim that interpretive/analytic activities are less substitutable. [Evaluating ChatGPT in Qualitative Thematic Analysis With Human Researchers](https://consensus.app/papers/details/eb41bc1341bf5cff8e49c87339481abe/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** This is the place in the chapter where the underlying claim is theoretically motivated rather than empirically tested at the specific level the chapter applies it. No peer-reviewed study has directly compared AI's impact on foundational vs advanced language-learning activities. The chapter is being responsible by framing this as "particularly vulnerable" and "may be more resilient" — appropriately hedged language. A small phrase like "as the cognitive offloading literature suggests, though direct comparative evidence is still emerging" would acknowledge the specific empirical state.

### Claim 8: We do not yet have long-term evidence about how extensive AI-supported learning affects language development over time
> "We do not yet have long-term evidence about how extensive AI-supported learning affects language development over time."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:** This is appropriately characterizing the state of the literature.
- All systematic reviews of AI in language learning to date (Teng 2024, Cong-Lem et al. 2025, others cited in earlier reports) explicitly note the lack of longitudinal evidence as a research gap. [A Systematic Review of ChatGPT for English as a Foreign Language Writing](https://consensus.app/papers/details/49e897c73d6c5b1a81051c65a139df46/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Klimova et al. (2024) explicitly states the limitation: "potential for developing research on MALL and reading comprehension for randomized control studies with larger populations and longer intervention periods" remains. [Use of Mobile Applications in Developing Reading Comprehension in Second Language Acquisition](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fce9546877ff5a00ad6043b859244c7f/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Solid. The chapter is correctly characterizing the state of evidence.

## Recommendations

1. **Fix the bibliography heading typo.** "Bibliograpahy" should be "Bibliography."

2. **Complete the Jain and Kiran (2025) citation.** The entry currently lacks a journal/venue.

3. **Consider adding a Roediger and Butler (2011) or Karpicke and Roediger (2007) citation** for Claim 5. These are the canonical citations for retrieval practice as a desirable difficulty and would directly support the chapter's argument about "retrieval, effortful recall, and the need to generate language under constraint" as conditions for learning. The retrieval-practice literature is unusually robust and worth anchoring explicitly.

4. **Consider adding a distributed cognition citation for Claim 6.** Hutchins (1995, *Cognition in the Wild*) or Zhang and Norman (1994) would anchor the framework directly. The chapter currently invokes distributed cognition by name but does not cite its theoretical sources.

5. **Soften Claim 7 slightly.** The vulnerability hierarchy (foundational vs advanced) is theoretically motivated and consistent with multiple research traditions, but no peer-reviewed study has directly compared AI's effects on foundational vs advanced language-learning activities. A small phrase like "as the cognitive offloading literature suggests, though direct comparative evidence is still emerging" would acknowledge the specific empirical state without weakening the argument.

6. **The chapter's epistemic caution is well-calibrated throughout.** Phrases like "research in this area is still emerging," "may be more resilient," and "we do not yet have long-term evidence" all match the actual state of the literature. No revisions needed for accuracy.

7. **The design heuristics in the second half of the chapter are practical extensions** of the underlying research and do not overclaim. The "design lens rather than shortcut" framing is appropriate.

---

## Research Gaps

- No peer-reviewed study has directly compared AI's effects on foundational vs advanced language-learning activities. The chapter's vulnerability hierarchy is theoretically grounded but empirically untested at this level of specificity.
- Long-term, longitudinal evidence on AI-supported language learning does not yet exist. The chapter correctly identifies this as a gap.
- The retrieval-practice literature has been extended to EFL (Aljabri 2024) but not directly tested in AI-augmented language-learning contexts. Whether AI use undermines the testing effect specifically is an open empirical question.
- The "illusion of competence" framing has been documented in writing, programming, and general cognitive tasks (Matueny 2025, Skulmowski 2023, Rao 2025) but not specifically tested on L2 oral production or unaided second-language performance after AI-assisted practice.
