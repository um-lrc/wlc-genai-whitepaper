# Accuracy Review: 07 - Pedagogical Approaches Three Years On

**Reviewed:** 2026-05-05
**Claims examined:** 5
**Overall verdict:** Well-supported

---

## Summary

This chapter introduces three pedagogical approaches to AI in language teaching: exploratory/reflective use, constrained/instrumental use, and critical abstention (non-use). Each is supported by substantial empirical literature. The exploratory-use claim has the strongest empirical anchor — multiple studies have now demonstrated that comparing AI output to student writing supports metalinguistic awareness, critical thinking, and digital literacy. The constrained-use claim is also well-grounded, with quasi-experimental evidence that AI used for brainstorming and feedback (paired with reflection) produces measurable writing gains. The critical-abstention claim follows logically from the cognitive offloading literature reviewed in earlier chapters. The chapter does not overreach. Like file 06, its bibliography is absent and would benefit from concrete additions.

## Claim-by-Claim Review

### Claim 1: Exploratory or reflective use of AI — analyzing AI output, comparing it to student writing, identifying limitations — supports metalinguistic awareness and critical reflection
> "Here, AI-generated language becomes an object of analysis rather than a substitute for student work. Students might compare AI output to their own writing, identify patterns or limitations, or discuss where meaning, tone, or cultural nuance is lost. Used this way, AI can support meta-linguistic awareness and critical reflection without replacing practice."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Lau (2024) makes precisely this argument from the language-teaching perspective: "Since language teachers are uniquely positioned to teach metalinguistic awareness, they can support their learners' understanding of how LLMs are shaped by language ideologies and how their outputs are indexical of social power. This awareness would help learners be more conscientious in using LLMs." [Is ChatGPT taking over the language classroom?](https://consensus.app/papers/details/3ad8176adb2a53aba89ae07bedd51238/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Tran and Nguyen (2023) qualitative study found that ChatGPT could support critical digital literacies in language learning, with both teachers and learners reporting growth in critical thinking and digital evaluation skills. [Exploring the Role of ChatGPT in Developing Critical Digital Literacies in Language Learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/222849727c765a97980ee23a651667f1/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Javier and Moorhouse (2023) describe a Filipino secondary EFL classroom intervention where students compared AI output to expected responses, identified factual inaccuracies, and discussed limitations as part of structured "experiential" lessons. The researchers observed measurable gains in students' critical evaluation of AI tools. [Developing secondary school English language learners' productive and critical use of ChatGPT](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b9f517b391c75273b1b113c4a65f6f41/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Li and Zhang (2023) describe a Chinese-language pedagogy where students "input assignment grading criteria into ChatGPT, which subsequently evaluates initial drafts," then "critically reflect on ChatGPT's initial draft evaluations and revise accordingly" — the precise reflective-use pattern the chapter is describing. [Exploring the Use of ChatGPT in Chinese Language Classrooms](https://consensus.app/papers/details/17938f0413325edf93eaa5da5f55907c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Massaro et al. (2023) directly tested ChatGPT's metalinguistic awareness on Italian and Italian dialect grammar, finding bias against reordered constructions and weakness with anaphoric clitics — exactly the kind of "patterns or limitations" the chapter says students can identify. [Prompting Metalinguistic Awareness in Large Language Models: ChatGPT and Bias Effects on the Grammar of Italian and Italian Varieties](https://consensus.app/papers/details/31066f10099053c6aba3e871dfba88dc/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Muñoz-Basols and Permuy Hércules de Solás (2023) propose a critical-ecological approach to applied translation that uses AI output as a starting point for L2 critical-thinking development, fitting the chapter's "object of analysis" framing. [Potentialities of Applied Translation for Language Learning in the Era of Artificial Intelligence](https://consensus.app/papers/details/60c9e36bd90a58858f23875e73dabe88/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** This claim has the strongest empirical anchor of any in the chapter. The literature on AI as object-of-analysis for metalinguistic awareness has grown substantially since 2023 and now includes both theoretical arguments (Lau 2024, Muñoz-Basols 2023) and direct classroom interventions (Javier & Moorhouse 2023, Li & Zhang 2023).

### Claim 2: Constrained or instrumental use — AI allowed for brainstorming, vocabulary exploration, or comparison but not final production — keeps emphasis on process rather than polish
> "AI might be permitted for brainstorming, vocabulary exploration, or comparison, but not for final production. The emphasis remains on process rather than polish, and students are encouraged to be explicit about how tools were used."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Ghafouri et al. (2024) tested a three-phase ChatGPT writing instruction protocol with 12 Iranian EFL teachers and 48 students: planning (AI for brainstorming), instruction (AI for engagement and reflection), and assessment. The intervention significantly improved learners' writing skills, with effects persisting over time — direct empirical support for the chapter's "AI as support, not stand-in" framing. [From virtual assistant to writing mentor: Exploring the impact of a ChatGPT-based writing instruction protocol on EFL teachers' self-efficacy and learners' writing skill](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b14c3eab2b1a5d33b567202f7afd43b3/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Anjarani and Sukyadi (2024) quasi-experimental study of 80 Indonesian EFL students found significantly higher engagement when ChatGPT was used for brainstorming and instructional support compared to a control group. [ChatGPT to Foster Students' Engagement in Writing Class: An Intervention Study](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1332fecb0a66548fabaf8bee364c25e8/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Wang (2024) phenomenological study of native and non-native English speakers found that students used ChatGPT primarily for brainstorming and organizing ideas, with the most successful integration involving conscious distinction between AI assistance and authorial voice. [Exploring Students' Generative AI-Assisted Writing Processes](https://consensus.app/papers/details/7ad4a53a307454ac8468036ad3a2341b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Hong et al. (2025), cited in earlier reports, demonstrated experimentally that AI-augmented pedagogy with structured cognitive offload (for lower-order tasks) and human focus on higher-order reflection produced significant critical-thinking gains. [Cognitive Offload Instruction with Generative AI](https://consensus.app/papers/details/78794fc5cb445c3ca4672712d936d817/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Liu et al. (2021) quasi-experimental study (50 vs 53 EFL writing students) found that AI-supported writing paired with reflective thinking promotion produced significantly better writing performance, self-efficacy, and self-regulated learning, while reducing cognitive load. [Incorporating a reflective thinking promoting mechanism into artificial intelligence-supported English writing environments](https://consensus.app/papers/details/079d62ef6589538ab70b4de0897a0c76/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Solid empirical anchor. The pattern the chapter describes (AI for process, with student responsible for product) is precisely what the most successful intervention studies have implemented. Ghafouri et al. (2024) is the single best citation if the chapter wants to anchor this claim with one study.

### Claim 3: Sometimes not using AI for a particular activity best supports learning, particularly when the goal is internalization, fluency, or real-time interaction
> "There are also situations where instructors decide that not using AI for a particular activity best supports learning. This choice is often most relevant when the goal is internalization, fluency development, or real-time interaction: contexts where the learning happens through effort, timing, and response."

**Support level:** Well-supported (by analogy to cognitive offloading research)

**Evidence:** This claim follows directly from the cognitive offloading and desirable difficulty literature examined in detail in the file 03 review. The relevant anchors:
- Bjork and Kroll (2015) on desirable difficulty in vocabulary and language learning. [Desirable Difficulties in Vocabulary Learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/d446782fcb4c5711885ec991a874de37/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Skulmowski (2023) on cognitive offloading creating "the illusion of having memorized that information" while underlying memory is weak. [The Cognitive Architecture of Digital Externalization](https://consensus.app/papers/details/c866680bf075553c83e92a5185f97d81/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Elzerman (2025) directly applies cognitive offloading concerns to language learning, arguing that AI tools "may contribute to cognitive offloading, reducing learners' reliance on their own memory and analytical skills, ultimately leading to dependency." [When AI Does the Thinking](https://consensus.app/papers/details/2799cbe0912d567d98f9ae0dd3a6eee1/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Saito et al. (2017) and Loewen and Sato (2018) on real-time interaction as a driver of L2 development.

**Notes:** "Critical abstention" is not a term that appears in the peer-reviewed literature with this specific meaning, and the chapter is appropriately cautious in framing it as one of multiple legitimate choices rather than as an established pedagogical movement. The underlying argument — that fluency, internalization, and real-time interaction depend on cognitive engagement that AI use can short-circuit — is well-grounded in the cognitive offloading and SLA traditions. As discussed in the file 03 review, no study has yet directly tested AI's effect on phonological development, grammatical automatization, or fluency in beginning language learners, so this claim remains theoretically grounded rather than empirically tested at the specific level the chapter applies it.

### Claim 4: Transparency about expectations helps students understand and reduces confusion
> "Instructors are most effective when they can explain, both to themselves and to students, what an activity is meant to develop, and why certain tools are or are not appropriate in that context. This transparency helps students understand expectations and reduces confusion about mixed messages."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Gonsalves (2024) found that 74% of students at King's Business School failed to declare AI use, with "fear of academic repercussions, ambiguous guidelines, inconsistent enforcement, and peer influence" as key barriers. The study explicitly recommends "clear, consistent, and trust-based policies" and "transparent communication" — direct support for the chapter's transparency claim. [Addressing student non-compliance in AI use declarations](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e8d863a0082658019ebd525a0845aaeb/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Alduais et al. (2025) policy analysis of 74 documents identified "transparency and disclosure" as one of five core domains for ethical GenAI policy across higher education, governmental, and publisher contexts. [Utilizing Generative AI Responsibly and Ethically for Research Purposes in Higher Education: A Policy Analysis](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f0599816c56959caae609cd76062da86/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Đerić et al. (2025) found across 823 participants that trust in GenAI tools is "experience-driven" and that "fostering appropriate trust through transparent policies, privacy safeguards, and practical training is critical." [Trust in Generative AI Tools: A Comparative Study of Higher Education Students, Teachers, and Researchers](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ee836bf85a7d5f1186db84e141676f5d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Well-grounded. The transparency claim is one of the most consistent findings in the institutional-policy literature on AI in higher education.

### Claim 5: Flexibility and recalibration are pedagogical strengths in this moment
> "These approaches are not fixed. Many instructors continue to experiment, revise, and recalibrate as they see how students respond and how tools evolve. Flexibility is a strength here, not a sign of uncertainty."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Van Horn (2024) studied 120 Korean university students over a 15-week semester and found that flexible, ongoing implementation strategies for ChatGPT integration produced sustained engagement and metacognitive awareness. The study explicitly recommends "flexibility in implementation strategies" as a finding. [ChatGPT in English Language Learning: Exploring Perceptions and Promoting Autonomy in a University EFL Context](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0341028eae805f88974da18f7c7907bd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Krist et al. (2023), cited in the file 06 review, showed that experienced teachers' moment-to-moment instructional decisions involved continuous recalibration based on student response — supporting the chapter's framing that flexibility is a feature of expert practice. [Which ideas, when, and why? An experienced teacher's in-the-moment pedagogical reasoning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/71eee5045584552bbfb266a1e3700b5d/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- The systematic review of ChatGPT in EFL writing by Teng (2024) recommends "a balanced approach to integrating AI tools like ChatGPT into writing curricula and creating a community of practice among teachers and students" — supporting iterative, flexible adaptation. [A Systematic Review of ChatGPT for English as a Foreign Language Writing](https://consensus.app/papers/details/49e897c73d6c5b1a81051c65a139df46/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** This claim is normative more than empirical, but is consistent with both the implementation-science literature on educational change and the specific findings on ChatGPT integration in language teaching.

## Recommendations

1. **Add a bibliography.** As with file 06, the chapter has none. The strongest specific additions:
   - **Lau (2024)** for the metalinguistic-awareness framing in Claim 1.
   - **Ghafouri et al. (2024)** for the constrained-use intervention model in Claim 2.
   - **Javier and Moorhouse (2023)** for the secondary-school L2 case study most closely matching the chapter's exploratory-use approach.
   - **Gonsalves (2024)** for the transparency and consistent-policy claim in Claim 4.

2. **Consider naming the three approaches more concretely.** The chapter currently labels them as "exploratory or reflective use," "constrained or instrumental use," and "critical abstention." The first two are well-aligned with how the literature describes these patterns, but "critical abstention" is the chapter's own coinage. The chapter is appropriately humble about this ("though it is best understood simply as one possible outcome of thoughtful instructional design"), which is the right move. No revision needed, but worth knowing that this term is not yet established in the literature.

3. **Consider a brief acknowledgment that the approaches can blend.** In practice, most successful integrations described in the literature combine all three approaches across different activities in the same course. Ghafouri et al. (2024) is a good example — AI used at planning and assessment phases, students writing independently in between. A small phrase noting that these approaches "are often combined within a single course" would strengthen the chapter's framing without disrupting its three-part structure.

4. **The chapter's framing about AI evolving is well-calibrated.** The acknowledgment that "many instructors continue to experiment, revise, and recalibrate as they see how students respond and how tools evolve" is consistent with the rapidly moving research base and avoids the "frozen-in-time" problem that some earlier chapters risk.

---

## Research Gaps

- No comparative-effectiveness study has yet directly tested whether the three approaches the chapter describes (exploratory, constrained, abstention) produce different learning outcomes when matched to different activity types. The chapter's framework is plausible and well-aligned with existing intervention studies, but the direct comparison is not yet in the peer-reviewed literature.
- "Critical abstention" specifically — the deliberate, principled choice not to use AI for a particular activity — has not been studied as a distinct pedagogical choice. The cognitive offloading and SLA literatures support the underlying logic, but the choice itself has not been examined as an intervention.
- The literature on transparent AI-use policies and student behavior is growing but largely correlational. Whether explicit, well-designed transparency improves actual disclosure rates and reduces misuse remains an open empirical question.
