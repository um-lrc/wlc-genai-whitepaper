# Accuracy Review: 10 - Three Years On - A Reassuring Close

**Reviewed:** 2026-05-05
**Claims examined:** 5
**Overall verdict:** Well-supported (synthesis of arguments examined in earlier reports)

---

## Summary

This is a closing/synthesis chapter rather than a chapter making new empirical claims. Most of its arguments have been examined in detail in earlier reports — the specific claims about AI's limits, the centrality of repair and interaction, and the importance of pedagogical alignment all rest on research bases already documented. The chapter is appropriately modest in its conclusions and avoids overreaching. The one claim that warrants the most direct attention is the empirical assertion that language education "has not been rendered obsolete" three years into widespread AI use — a claim that is consistent with the recent literature on language teaching adaptation but is also a moving target. The chapter handles this well by framing the change as one of "clarity" rather than survival.

---

## Claim-by-Claim Review

### Claim 1: Three years into widespread generative AI use, language education has not been rendered obsolete
> "Three years into widespread generative AI use, the most important lesson may be this: language education has not been rendered obsolete, nor has its purpose fundamentally changed."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- Moorhouse and Wan (2024) interviewed 13 English language teacher educators across all four Hong Kong government-funded universities. While participants believed GenAI tools "will substantially affect the ILTE curriculum, instruction, and assessment," none anticipated the obsolescence of language teacher education. The framing throughout was adaptation rather than replacement. [The effects of generative AI on initial language teacher education](https://consensus.app/papers/details/bfc7db67f8665dfb8460768ed4af9bdd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Cong-Lem et al. (2025) systematic review of 22 EFL studies on GenAI and equity (cited in earlier reports) found extensive evidence of pedagogical adaptation rather than disruption to the field's core. [Bridging or Breaking? A Systematic Review of How Generative AI Shapes Equity in Foreign Language Education](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ea83fe1192ee5872a0b6e276ec7dda4c/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Chan and Hu (2023) survey of 399 students in Hong Kong found "generally positive attitude towards GenAI in teaching and learning," with students seeing GenAI as a complement to existing pedagogy rather than its replacement. [Students' voices on generative AI: perceptions, benefits, and challenges in higher education](https://consensus.app/papers/details/1126e0bb4c775962a7ae377a2c21a1a7/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Haroud et al. (2025) found that both teachers and students agreed that GenAI "cannot replace the essential human role of educators in providing personalized guidance and addressing students' emotional and cognitive needs." [Generative AI in Higher Education: Teachers' and Students' Perspectives on Support, Replacement, and Digital Literacy](https://consensus.app/papers/details/ca9f651f02965df495d8e8bc4a613e4f/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** This is the chapter's most distinctive empirical claim. It is well-supported, but it is also a moving target. As AI capabilities continue to evolve, the appropriate framing may shift. The chapter handles this gracefully by emphasizing that "what has changed is the clarity with which we are asked to name what matters" — a framing that does not depend on AI's future capabilities.

### Claim 2: Generative AI can produce language quickly and fluently, but cannot replace the experience of learning to mean something in another language
> "Generative AI can produce language quickly and fluently, but it cannot replace the experience of learning to mean something in another language."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:**
- The first half of the claim (AI's fluent language production) is empirically uncontested.
- The second half rests on the cognitive offloading and SLA literatures examined in detail in the file 03 review. The "illusion of competence" literature (Matueny et al. 2025), Skulmowski (2023), and Gerlich (2025) all support the claim that AI-produced language can mask the absence of underlying learning. [Illusion of Competence and Skill Degradation in Artificial Intelligence Dependency among Users](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a98917a1497b56a0829d051456557623/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Sakaguchi et al. (2025) and the broader interpretive-AI literature, examined in the file 04 review, demonstrate that AI handles surface features competently but loses cultural and embodied meaning. [Evaluating ChatGPT in Qualitative Thematic Analysis With Human Researchers](https://consensus.app/papers/details/eb41bc1341bf5cff8e49c87339481abe/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Solid synthesis claim. The framing "experience of learning to mean something" is rhetorically apt and aligns with both the cognitive offloading evidence and the SLA tradition's emphasis on meaning-negotiation as central to language acquisition.

### Claim 3: AI cannot stand in for misunderstanding and repair, for negotiating tone and context, or for the slow confidence that comes from sustained use
> "It cannot stand in for misunderstanding and repair, for negotiating tone and context, or for the slow confidence that comes from sustained use."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:** This claim consolidates findings examined in earlier reports.
- The repair and negotiation-of-meaning literature (Saito et al. 2017, Loewen and Sato 2018, Yan et al. 2025, Cao 2024) supports the first part. [Video-Based Interaction, Negotiation for Comprehensibility, and Second Language Speech Learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/01099e5b15d85b9986611cd4b3b9d90b/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- The L2 self and motivation literature (Dörnyei and Ushioda 2009, Al-Hoorie 2018, Norton) supports the "negotiating tone and context" claim. [The L2 motivational self system: A meta-analysis](https://consensus.app/papers/details/01e28044244550cb9b837a3a3e493c54/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- The desirable difficulty literature (Bjork and Bjork 2011, Bjork and Kroll 2015) and longitudinal interaction studies (Hanzawa 2021) support the "slow confidence that comes from sustained use" framing. [Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e7fef89288255cb090540e0a025dd48a/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** This is rhetorically powerful and empirically grounded. The three things named (repair, tone-negotiation, slow confidence) each track a documented research tradition.

### Claim 4: What endures is the need for alignment between learning goals, instructional design, assessment, and communication
> "There is no single policy that will fit every course, no final stance that will hold forever. What does endure is the need for alignment: between learning goals, instructional design, assessment, and communication with students."

**Support level:** Well-supported

**Evidence:** This is the constructive alignment / backward design framing examined in the file 06 review.
- Biggs (1999) — the foundational statement of constructive alignment. [Teaching for Quality Learning at University: What the Student Does](https://consensus.app/papers/details/74607c0ebcb151b3aca67958664123dd/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- Hailikari et al. (2021) — empirical evidence that constructively aligned teaching guides students toward deep approaches to learning. [Student perspectives on how different elements of constructive alignment support active learning](https://consensus.app/papers/details/f2b957a062e35e108cedbcf7a33a4290/?utm_source=claude_desktop)
- MacPhail et al. (2021) on instructional alignment as a practical framework. [Promoting instructional alignment in physical education teacher education](https://consensus.app/papers/details/8a8a2212a5f35eb7b51f15afc5a72a29/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

**Notes:** Well-grounded but, like file 06, the chapter could benefit from naming this tradition explicitly. A reader unfamiliar with backward design might mistake the alignment claim as a novel framing rather than a well-established principle.

### Claim 5: Language education has always asked students to slow down, to attend carefully, and to remain open to difference
> "Language education has always asked students to slow down, to attend carefully, and to remain open to difference. In that sense, the work continues much as it always has."

**Support level:** Not directly researchable

**Notes:** This is a normative/philosophical claim about the disciplinary identity of language education rather than an empirically testable hypothesis. It is consistent with sociocultural theory (Lantolf), the L2-self framework (Dörnyei and Ushioda), and the broader applied-linguistics tradition's emphasis on intercultural competence (Kramsch 2014, cited in earlier reports). [Teaching Foreign Languages in an Era of Globalization: Introduction](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a5b2641b0f2f5e8082a47f47eedf60d4/?utm_source=claude_desktop)

The chapter is being appropriately modest in framing this as a closing reflection rather than a research finding.

## Recommendations

1. **The chapter is appropriately concise and modest.** As a closing piece, it does not need a full bibliography. A small set of 2-3 anchor citations would, however, ground its thesis statements:
   - For Claim 1, Moorhouse and Wan (2024) on language teacher educators' perceptions
   - For Claim 4, a single Biggs (1999) or Wiggins and McTighe (2005) citation to anchor the alignment framing
   - For Claim 3, one synthesis citation pointing back to repair and interaction (Saito et al. 2017 or Loewen and Sato 2018)

2. **Consider adding one sentence of temporal framing.** The "three years on" framing is rhetorically strong. The chapter might benefit from a brief acknowledgment that "this assessment is provisional in a fast-moving field" or similar language that signals the chapter understands its conclusions are not final. The chapter already gestures at this with "no final stance that will hold forever," which is good.

3. **The chapter's central rhetorical move is well-calibrated.** The framing that "what has changed is the clarity with which we are asked to name what matters" is empirically defensible across both stable and changing AI capabilities, and is a useful thesis for a closing chapter.

4. **No revisions needed for accuracy.** Every claim that can be empirically tested is supported by literature already cited in earlier reports.

---

## Research Gaps

- Long-term, longitudinal evidence on how language teaching has adapted to AI does not yet exist — most studies are cross-sectional or limited to a single semester. The chapter's "three years on" framing is appropriate but could be strengthened by future longitudinal work.
- Comparative effectiveness studies on different AI integration approaches (the typology developed in file 07) have not yet produced clear empirical guidance about which approaches produce better learning outcomes for which students.
- The disciplinary-identity claim in Claim 5 is implicitly empirical (does language education actually maintain its core character under AI conditions?) but is not directly testable. Sociolinguistic and ethnographic studies of language classrooms post-AI would be useful here.
